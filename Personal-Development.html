Reflection: "7 Cures for a Lean Purse"

In George S. Clason's The Richest Man in Babylon, Arkad outlines seven fundamental steps to attain financial security. The cure that resonated most with me is the first: "Start thy purse to fattening."

As a university student, it is easy to assume that saving money is impossible until graduation. However, Arkad emphasizes that retaining at least one-tenth of whatever you earn—regardless of how modest—creates a foundation of discipline. Applying this principle means setting aside 10% of my monthly stipend or freelance earnings into an untouchable savings fund before budgeting for groceries or leisure. Controlling expenditures and making savings work through compounding interest transforms small habits into substantial financial independence.

Reflection: "5 Laws of Gold"

The 5 Laws of Gold emphasize wisdom over reckless speculation. Gold comes gladly and in increasing quantity to those who put it to good use, but it flees from anyone who forces it into impossible earnings or trusts the advice of inexperienced individuals.

This principle applies directly to young adults navigating modern financial trends. In our digital age, get-rich-quick schemes, unregulated speculative trading, and high-risk gambles are heavily marketed to young people. Clason reminds us that wealth grows under the guidance of wise custodians who understand risk management. For me, this means seeking financial counsel from qualified professionals and verified resources rather than social media influencers.

My Biggest Takeaway

The overarching lesson from The Richest Man in Babylon is that financial stability is governed by habits, self-control, and patience, not merely the size of your income. Skill and discipline must precede wealth; if one cannot manage a small budget, larger sums will vanish just as quickly. Developing personal responsibility as an undergraduate will prepare me to handle professional compensation wisely once I graduate.
